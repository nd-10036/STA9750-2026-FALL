For the 2027 Netflix content strategy, we recommend to focus on developping globally available titles, while retaining quality in local catalogs. We also recommend developing more Live Events to drive adjacent revenues generated by high short-lived, event-driven viewership.

Given the preparation for the 2027 budget, the What We Watched Reports (2023H1-2026H1) were used to analyze past trends on key metrics (hours viewed, global/regional availability, and content typology). Overall, the Netflix viewership has increased over the periods since 2023H2, with a 13% growth, as noted in the graph below. The goal is to find the right strategy to continue the upward trajectory in viewership on a long-term basis.

The Netflix briefing backs this fact, stating that 55% of viewership are Originals (in 2023H1, Rev, 2023). However, as seen above, we note that top global titles in terms of hours viewed also contain licensed shows with worldwide rights: King the Land, Queen of Tears and Pablo Escobar (What’s on Netflix, 2023; CJ Newsroom, 2024; Wikipedia, n.d.-b).

Focusing on developing global Original content and acquiring globally licensed content will most likely support the continuity in the upward viewership trajectory.

A typical (median) title performs as well for both categories, with 1.9M for Live Events vs. 1.8M for Pre-taped in hours viewed. However, the average title is higher in terms of hours viewed for Pre-taped, with 21.1M vs. 3.9M for Live Events. It stands to reason because Pre-taped titles have a longer historical presence in Netflix’s catalog history and big hits (e.g. Stranger Things) drive the average up, thus having a higher viewership.

Another calculation further supports this argument: the Gini Coefficient, which is commonly used to assess an inequality in any given distribution (with 0% having no inequality and 100% being a perfect inequality). The calculation measures the inequality in the share of viewership across titles. The metric Views was used as it’s the closest at estimating how many people watched a title. The results show a high, stable inequality in the viewership with a minimum of 70.0% and a maximum of 71.8% across studied periods (2023H2-2026H1). This calculation thus concludes that high popularity content (across all content types) seizes most viewership in the catalog.

Live Events have shown their capabilities in attracting advertisers and sponsors. Netflix backs this argument by stating that such events bring “outsized value” - for example, the Paul vs. Tyson fight is the “most-streamed sporting event ever” (Netflix, 2025). Sources also mention that Netflix “has sold out of advertising inventory” (The Hollywood Reporter, 2024) during two Christmas Day NFL games, with partnerships and in-game segments.

The proposed strategy is to increase “one-shot” Live Events to bring one-off, high-value attention, and increase revenue around advertising and sponsorship - all while complementing Pre-taped content by continuing to enrich historical content, which brings stable and long-tail viewership.

Basing recommendations solely on hours viewed can bias the results, as viewership varies largely based on typology of content (movies vs. TV shows vs. one-shot Live Events). Furthermore, deeper analysis on world-region viewership cannot be done at this time, which limits the analysis of global vs. regional content popularity.

The hours viewed alone for a given title doesn’t measure its success (Netflix, 2023). The reports are not compared against any revenue streams (income vs. spending), making it hard to propose solid recommendations by analyzing the viewership vs. associated costs. Finally, a part of non-English titles was dropped in this analysis (noting that all non-English titles accounted for 30% of viewership in the first published report 2023H1, Netflix, 2023).

Proposed next steps include:

  1. Cross-reference viewership data with revenue streams (revenue and costs) to definitively assess successful content streams
  2. Conduct an analysis of the catalog, broken down by world region against global availability to assess the impact of scaling up development of globally available titles, while retaining and increasing regionally licensed content viewership
  3. Conduct an exploratory analysis of past Live Events to assess popularity, revenues and performance to determine what typology to eventually develop more

1/ Introduction

The following analysis deep dives into Netflix’s What We Watched reports, a Netflix strategic initiative offering insights into their viewership. These reports have different goals, including identifying trends on content, viewership, popularity, catalog content. Based on these reports, we develop a strategy memo for Netflix’s leadership, to inform on the 2027 content strategy.

What is the Netflix What We Watched Report? (Netflix, 2023)

  • Reports published every 6 months starting in 2023
  • Aggregate total global viewership for almost the entire Netflix catalog over six-month periods
  • Key indicators include:
  1. Hours viewed for every title - original and licensed - watched for over 50,000 hours;
  2. The premiere date for any Netflix TV series or film; and
  3. Whether a title was available globally.

2/ Data Ingest and Cleaning

The very first step is to import the data and transform it to make it efficiently usable for analysis.

After importing the data:

Show import and cleaning code 
#' Acquire Netflix What We Watched Reports
#'
#' This function will acquire and standardize all data for MP#01
#' from Netflix. Because Netflix hosts the data files behind a CDN, we
#' are accessing the release pages and automatically extracting links to the
#' relevant files (with CDN hash).
#'
#' After import, files are stacked vertically and some basic cleaning is
#' performed.


load_netflix_data <- function() {
  # Note to students: Make sure you have installed the tidyverse and fs packages
  #                   before beginning this assignment. If you aren't sure,
  #                   simply run `library(tidyverse)` at your R Console and, if
  #                   you don't see an error message, `tidyverse` has been
  #                   installed. (Same for `fs`) If you still need to install
  #                   either package, use the `install.packages` function as
  #                   demonstrated in class.
  library(tidyverse)
  library(rvest)
  library(readxl)
  library(fs)

  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")

  if (!dir_exists(data_dir)) {
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings = FALSE, recurse = TRUE)
  }

  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )

  download_if_needed <- function(url, local) {
    if (!file_exists(local)) {
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }

    return(local)
  }

  NETFLIX_URLS |>
    map(read_html) |>
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |>
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |>
    enframe("period", "url") |>
    mutate(
      path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")),
      path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed),
      data = map(path, read_excel, skip = 3, na = "*"),
      data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))
    ) |>
    unnest(data) |>
    select(-url, -path) |>
    mutate(
      year = str_sub(period, 1, 4),
      half = str_sub(period, start = 6),
      period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half == 1, 1, 7), 1),
      period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half == 1, 6, 12), if_else(half == 1, 30, 31))
    ) |>
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |>
    mutate(
      live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"),
      clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
      Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))
    ) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(
      Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm = TRUE)),
      .by = Title,
      Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
      Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"),
      Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"),
      Title = str_trim(Title)
    ) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(
      year,
      period,
      period_start,
      period_end,
      Title,
      -half,
      Runtime,
      Views,
      `Hours Viewed`,
      `Release Date`,
      `Available Globally?`,
      live_event
    ) |>
    rename(
      title = Title,
      runtime = Runtime,
      views = Views,
      hours_viewed = `Hours Viewed`,
      release_date = `Release Date`,
      available_globally = `Available Globally?`
    ) |>
    mutate(
      year = as.integer(year),
      available_globally = available_globally == "Yes"
    ) |>
    filter_out(title == "Other Shows")
}

NETFLIX_DATA <- load_netflix_data()
  1. we rename columns in the data set to set valid R variable names, aligned on the same format (e.g. all lower case, multi-word separated by underscores)
  2. we change variable types when appropriate, to ensure that R will interpret them as the correct type for future arithmetic manipulation (e.g. year from chr to int)
  3. we filter out the “Other Shows” entries from the 2025H2 and 2026H1 reports. Netflix changed the report by aggregating many shows together under one bucket, thus not representing a single one. This would cause a distortion in title rankings and comparisons across years/periods.

Show Gini Coefficient formula code 
gini <- function(y) {
  y <- sort(y[!is.na(y)])
  n <- length(y)
  if (n == 0) {
    return(NA)
  }
  (2 / n) * sum(seq_len(n) * y) / sum(y) - (n + 1) / n
}

The Gini Coefficient is a mathematical formula used to measure inequality in a given distribution. The best example is when measuring inequality in income distribution (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The math is simple: the lower the coefficient, the more equal the distribution is. The same logic works the other way around; the higher the coefficient, the higher the inequality. The percentage range in the output is: 0 representing a perfect equality and 100 a perfect inequality, where one value takes it all.

Below is an example of the output the formula will give, on two different sets:

  1. [10, 10, 10, 10, 10], gives a coefficient of: 0%

  2. [0, 0, 0, 0, 100], gives a coefficient of: 80%

The first set shows perfect equality, with 0%, in both the values and the coefficient, with only tens. When we change the set with only zeros and a 100, the coefficient climbs to 80%, demonstrating high inequality in the set. Since there are only 5 values, the maximum coefficient can only reach 80%.

We test another example. Suppose a country has only 5 citizens, and their incomes are:

  1. Citizen: [Alice, Barbara, Carol, Debbie, Emily]
  2. Income: [20k, 200k, 0k, 50k, 40k]

We run the coefficient and find 55.5%. This result indicates a fairly high inequality in the income data set (which stands to reason when looking at the income with opposites 0k and 200k).

Now, we take a look at the Netflix data set. There are two different ways we can run the coefficient:

  1. By hours viewed - the global aggregate view time (in hours) during the period of a given title (appears to be rounded to the nearest 100,000 hours and low viewership shows, with titles with less than 50,000 hours being omitted)
  2. By views - the approximate number of views, rounded and computed by dividing Hours Viewed by Runtime (which assumes that all viewers watched a title in its entirety, with obvious limitations).

Assessing by Views could be considered as more accurate, since it evaluates a title in a given period by trying to get the number of people who watched one title (excluding the length difference between a movie and a TV show). On the other hand, Hours Viewed will be better at evaluating the time spent on the platform (although it will not differentiate the popularity vs. the length). Both serve a different purpose, and both are valuable.

Since the two variables appear to have limitations, we run and analyze both in the Gini coefficient.

Note: again, since views data were added starting 2023H2, we omit the 2023H1 data.

Based on the results, the inequality comes mostly from popularity and not length (since the views estimation removes the length issue). The Views Gini results are only 3.8 percentage points lower than Gini results for Hours Viewed. We conclude that popular titles (no matter the origin) have a tendency to dominate Netflix’s viewership.

7/ Conclusion

The data in the What We Watched Netflix reports proved to be valuable. It brought key insights into data quality, catalog substance, viewership and content typology that allows us to make estimations for future content strategies. Main findings include:

  1. Growth: we note a steady growth in viewership over the years, broken down by periods (since 2023H2)
  2. Viewership Concentration: gravitates around popular titles that absorb the majority of viewership
  3. Globally vs. Regionally available content: global content leads in viewership despite not being the main type available in the catalog
  4. Live Events vs. Pre-taped content: Live Events attract high one-shot viewership and adjacent revenue, while Pre-taped content ensures high viewership through big hits

Although limited in cross-reference data (such as revenue streams, world presence breakdowns etc.), we get a sense of what is popular on the platform, what customers actually watch, and what direction the content may take.

This analysis once again proved how valuable it is to analyze performance based on real data, to understand and offer adapted content that will bring novelty and retain loyalty for a given product.

### 1/ Introduction

The following analysis deep dives into Netflix's What We Watched reports, a Netflix strategic initiative offering insights into their viewership. These reports have different goals, including identifying trends on content, viewership, popularity, catalog content. Based on these reports, we develop a strategy memo for Netflix's leadership, to inform on the 2027 content strategy.

***What is the Netflix What We Watched Report?*** (Netflix, 2023)

- Reports published every 6 months starting in 2023
- Aggregate total global viewership for almost the entire Netflix catalog over six-month periods
- Key indicators include:

1. Hours viewed for every title - original and licensed - watched for over 50,000 hours; 
2. The premiere date for any Netflix TV series or film; and 
3. Whether a title was available globally. 

### 2/ Data Ingest and Cleaning

The very first step is to import the data and transform it to make it efficiently usable for analysis.

After importing the data:

```{r}

#| warning: false
#| code-summary: "Show import and cleaning code"
#' Acquire Netflix What We Watched Reports
#'
#' This function will acquire and standardize all data for MP#01
#' from Netflix. Because Netflix hosts the data files behind a CDN, we
#' are accessing the release pages and automatically extracting links to the
#' relevant files (with CDN hash).
#'
#' After import, files are stacked vertically and some basic cleaning is
#' performed.


load_netflix_data <- function() {
  # Note to students: Make sure you have installed the tidyverse and fs packages
  #                   before beginning this assignment. If you aren't sure,
  #                   simply run `library(tidyverse)` at your R Console and, if
  #                   you don't see an error message, `tidyverse` has been
  #                   installed. (Same for `fs`) If you still need to install
  #                   either package, use the `install.packages` function as
  #                   demonstrated in class.
  library(tidyverse)
  library(rvest)
  library(readxl)
  library(fs)

  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")

  if (!dir_exists(data_dir)) {
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings = FALSE, recurse = TRUE)
  }

  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )

  download_if_needed <- function(url, local) {
    if (!file_exists(local)) {
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }

    return(local)
  }

  NETFLIX_URLS |>
    map(read_html) |>
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |>
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |>
    enframe("period", "url") |>
    mutate(
      path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")),
      path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed),
      data = map(path, read_excel, skip = 3, na = "*"),
      data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))
    ) |>
    unnest(data) |>
    select(-url, -path) |>
    mutate(
      year = str_sub(period, 1, 4),
      half = str_sub(period, start = 6),
      period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half == 1, 1, 7), 1),
      period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half == 1, 6, 12), if_else(half == 1, 30, 31))
    ) |>
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |>
    mutate(
      live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"),
      clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
      Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))
    ) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(
      Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm = TRUE)),
      .by = Title,
      Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
      Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"),
      Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"),
      Title = str_trim(Title)
    ) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(
      year,
      period,
      period_start,
      period_end,
      Title,
      -half,
      Runtime,
      Views,
      `Hours Viewed`,
      `Release Date`,
      `Available Globally?`,
      live_event
    ) |>
    rename(
      title = Title,
      runtime = Runtime,
      views = Views,
      hours_viewed = `Hours Viewed`,
      release_date = `Release Date`,
      available_globally = `Available Globally?`
    ) |>
    mutate(
      year = as.integer(year),
      available_globally = available_globally == "Yes"
    ) |>
    filter_out(title == "Other Shows")
}

NETFLIX_DATA <- load_netflix_data()
```

1. we rename columns in the data set to set valid `R` variable names, aligned on the same format (e.g. all lower case, multi-word separated by underscores)
2. we change variable types when appropriate, to ensure that `R` will interpret them as the correct type for future arithmetic manipulation (e.g. `year` from `chr` to `int`)
3. we filter out the "Other Shows" entries from the 2025H2 and 2026H1 reports. Netflix changed the report by aggregating many shows together under one bucket, thus not representing a single one. This would cause a distortion in title rankings and comparisons across years/periods.

What about the share of shows vs. movies? The raw data set doesn't give that information. To find the answer, we define two criteria that will be used for the output:

1. Length: anything over ~4 hours is almost certainly a TV show, not a movie
2. "Season": if a title has "Season" or "Limited Series" in it, it is almost certainly a TV show

It is important to note that:

1. As previously noted, there is a high number of missing values in the run time (especially in 2023H1). We therefore use a criterion to classify titles, with missing run time, with keywords only
2. The way Netflix names titles is a bit inconsistent in how it represents different seasons of TV shows (e.g. Seinfeld: Season 1 and Seinfeld: Season 3, but Stranger Things and Stranger Things 3). The ratios may therefore be limited, if the classification is solely based on title names

```{r}

# Rule to classify a title as a TV show vs. a movie:
# "Season" or "Limited Series" in title, or run time over 4 hours
is_show <- function(title, runtime) {
  str_detect(title, "Season|Limited Series") | coalesce(runtime > 240, FALSE)
}

shows_movies <- NETFLIX_DATA |>
  mutate(type = if_else(is_show(title, runtime), "Shows", "Movies")) |>

  select(period, type, share) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = type, values_from = share)

shows_movies |>
  gt() |>

    subtitle = "Overall stable distribution across shows and movies, with shows leading in the race"
  ) |>
  cols_label(period = "Period") |>
  fmt_percent(columns = c(Shows, Movies), decimals = 1) |>
  tab_source_note("Note: TV show/movie classified based on title keywords and run time over 4 hours.") |>
  tab_footnote(
    footnote = "High number of run time values missing in 2023H1, classification based on title keywords only.",
    locations = cells_body(
      columns = c(Shows, Movies),
      rows = period == "2023H1"
    )
  ) |>
  tab_source_note("Source: Netflix - What We Watched reports, 2023H1-2026H1") |>
```

We note a common pattern across all periods, with an average of **`r scales::percent(mean(shows_movies$Shows[shows_movies$period != "2023H1"]), accuracy = 0.1)`** viewing time for shows and **`r scales::percent(mean(shows_movies$Movies[shows_movies$period != "2023H1"]), accuracy = 0.1)`** for movies. We exclude 2023H1 from these averages for the same reason mentioned above (**`r scales::percent(missing_2023h1, accuracy = 1)`** missing run time). 

Based on the criteria, titles with missing run times are classified based solely on the presence of keywords such as "season". This may expose an issue in the ratio shows vs. movies in 2023H1 versus all other periods.

### 6/ Extra Analysis: the Gini coefficient

```{r}
#| label: ec-gini-function
#| code-summary: "Show Gini Coefficient formula code"
gini <- function(y) {
  y <- sort(y[!is.na(y)])
  n <- length(y)
  if (n == 0) {
    return(NA)
  }
  (2 / n) * sum(seq_len(n) * y) / sum(y) - (n + 1) / n
}
```

The Gini Coefficient is a mathematical formula used to measure inequality in a given distribution. The best example is when measuring inequality in income distribution (Wikipedia, n.d.-a). The math is simple: the lower the coefficient, the more equal the distribution is. The same logic works the other way around; the higher the coefficient, the higher the inequality. The percentage range in the output is: 0 representing a perfect equality and 100 a perfect inequality, where one value takes it all.

Below is an example of the output the formula will give, on two different sets:

1. [10, 10, 10, 10, 10], gives a coefficient of: **`r scales::percent(gini(c(10, 10, 10, 10, 10)), accuracy = 1)`**

2. [0, 0, 0, 0, 100], gives a coefficient of: **`r scales::percent(gini(c(0, 0, 0, 0, 100)), accuracy = 1)`**

The first set shows perfect equality, with **`r scales::percent(gini(c(10, 10, 10, 10, 10)), accuracy = 1)`**, in both the values and the coefficient, with only tens. When we change the set with only zeros and a 100, the coefficient climbs to **`r scales::percent(gini(c(0, 0, 0, 0, 100)), accuracy = 1)`**, demonstrating high inequality in the set. Since there are only 5 values, the maximum coefficient can only reach **`r scales::percent(gini(c(0, 0, 0, 0, 100)), accuracy = 1)`**.

We test another example. Suppose a country has only 5 citizens, and their incomes are:

1. Citizen: [Alice, Barbara, Carol, Debbie, Emily]
2. Income: [20k, 200k, 0k, 50k, 40k]

We run the coefficient and find **`r scales::percent(gini(c(20, 200, 0, 50, 40)), accuracy = 0.1)`**. This result indicates a fairly high inequality in the income data set (which stands to reason when looking at the income with opposites 0k and 200k).

Now, we take a look at the Netflix data set. There are two different ways we can run the coefficient:

1. By hours viewed - the global aggregate view time (in hours) during the period of a given title (appears to be rounded to the nearest 100,000 hours and low viewership shows, with titles with less than 50,000 hours being omitted)
2. By views - the approximate number of views, rounded and computed by dividing Hours Viewed by Runtime (which assumes that all viewers watched a title in its entirety, with obvious limitations).

Assessing by Views could be considered as more accurate, since it evaluates a title in a given period by trying to get the number of people who watched one title (excluding the length difference between a movie and a TV show). On the other hand, Hours Viewed will be better at evaluating the time spent on the platform (although it will not differentiate the popularity vs. the length). Both serve a different purpose, and both are valuable. 

Since the two variables appear to have limitations, we run and analyze both in the Gini coefficient.

*Note:* again, since views data were added starting 2023H2, we omit the 2023H1 data.

No matter what the variable, all outputs from the Gini Coefficient show a stable, high inequality in the data. The lowest score for Hours Viewed is **`r scales::percent(min(gini_by_period$gini_hours), accuracy = 0.1)`** in **`r gini_by_period$period[which.min(gini_by_period$gini_hours)]`** and reaches up to **`r scales::percent(max(gini_by_period$gini_hours), accuracy = 0.1)`** in **`r gini_by_period$period[which.max(gini_by_period$gini_hours)]`**. Similarly, the lowest score for Views is **`r scales::percent(min(gini_by_period$gini_views), accuracy = 0.1)`** in **`r gini_by_period$period[which.min(gini_by_period$gini_views)]`** and reaches up to **`r scales::percent(max(gini_by_period$gini_views), accuracy = 0.1)`** in **`r gini_by_period$period[which.max(gini_by_period$gini_views)]`**.

But why compare Hours Viewed and Views? Hours Viewed will systematically be higher than Views (as explained earlier). Views will favor reach over quantity, in terms of how many people watched a title, removing the inequality in length of a TV show vs. a movie.

Based on the results, the inequality comes mostly from popularity and not length (since the views estimation removes the length issue). The Views Gini results are only **`r scales::number(mean(gini_by_period$gini_hours - gini_by_period$gini_views) * 100, accuracy = 0.1)`** percentage points lower than Gini results for Hours Viewed. We conclude that popular titles (no matter the origin) have a tendency to dominate Netflix's viewership.
